# Episode design: Great Zimbabwe, 1871 (the city of stone)

Status: **design draft** (2026-10-01), for the developer's approval before
anything is built. Shelf III (opens after 4 tales), Africa, from the plan
of 16 tales (`docs/stillroom_frame.md`, *The full shelf*). It replaces the
sealed teaser `sealed_zimbabwe_1871` and closes shelf III. Proposed id:
`great_zimbabwe_1871`; jar label **Great Zimbabwe, 1871**, its map pin on
the ruins near Masvingo.

## Premise

On the plateau of south-eastern Zimbabwe stand the ruins of a city of
stone: a hill crowned with walled enclosures, and below it the Great
Enclosure, an oval wall some 250 m round and up to 11 m high, laid in dry
granite without mortar, a band of chevrons along its top and a solid
conical tower inside. The ancestors of the **Shona** built it from the
**11th century** on; at its height in the 13th to 15th centuries it was
the seat of a kingdom that traded gold and ivory to the Swahili coast,
and Chinese porcelain, Persian ware, glass beads, and a copper coin of
Kilwa have been found in it. *Dzimba dza mabwe*, "houses of stone", gave
the country its name in 1980.

In **September 1871** the German geologist **Karl Mauch**, led there by the
hunter Adam Render, reached the ruins. He could not believe that people of
the land had built them. He cut a splinter from a fallen wooden lintel,
found it reddish, scented, untouched by insects, and very like the wood
of his pencil: cedar, he decided, from Lebanon, which only the Phoenicians
could have brought. The builder, he wrote, could have been none other than
the **Queen of Sheba**; this was **Ophir**, where Solomon's gold came from.
The wood was **tambootie** (*Spirostachys africana*), a scented African
hardwood that insects leave alone.

The legend served the colony that followed. In 1889 a hunter cut away one
of the eight soapstone birds and sold it to Cecil Rhodes; in 1891 Theodore
Bent took four more. In 1902 the curator Richard Hall dug out some twelve
feet of deposits to free the site of the "filth and decadence of the
Kaffir occupation", throwing away the evidence of who had lived there.
Archaeologists said otherwise: David Randall-MacIver in **1905**, Gertrude
Caton-Thompson in **1929** ("every detail ... seems African Bantu"). In
**1970** the Rhodesian government forbade official publications to state
it as fact, and the archaeologist Peter Garlake left in protest. The last
of the birds came home in April 2026.

The keeper's jar holds the builders, the century, and the wood.

## Tone and guardrails

- **The builders are honoured, not shown.** No people of the city are
  given faces or names; their work (the walls, the chevrons, the birds)
  speaks. Echoes are faceless: a builder carrying a stone, from the city's
  time.
- **Mauch is told by his own words, not mocked.** His notebook, his pencil,
  his splinter; the error is shown by the wood, not by ridicule. Render and
  Mauch are real and named; no one living is shown.
- **The colonial uses of the legend are stated plainly** (Rhodes's bird,
  Hall's clearing, the 1970 censorship), in later papers, without
  dramatising.
- **No sacred claim is made for the birds** beyond what is published (they
  stood on monoliths on the hill; they are read as birds of prey, perhaps
  bateleurs; they are the national emblem).
- **Legends are red herrings:** the Queen of Sheba, Ophir, the Phoenicians,
  cedar of Lebanon, the "10th century BC" of Solomon.

## Where it is set

- **A. The ruins in September 1871** (recommended): overgrown walls, fig
  trees rooted in them, the msasa trees red with new leaves; Mauch's camp
  with his notebook and pencil, the fallen lintel at a doorway. The later
  papers (Hall, Randall-MacIver, Caton-Thompson, 1970, the birds' return)
  arrive as papers "that should not be here yet". The tale's own year is
  the legend's birth, and the wood that disproves it is still lying there.
- **B. The city at its height, about 1350**: the walls being raised, the
  trade goods arriving; Mauch's notebook arrives from the future. Gives
  the builders their moment, but the lintel test and the legend sit
  awkwardly in a living city.

## New mechanics (this tale only)

- **The courses (`courses`).** The height of the tale. A breach in the
  outer wall, and a pile of fallen granite blocks of different lengths.
  Lay them back course by course, dry, no mortar: each course must fill
  the gap exactly, and no joint may sit over a joint in the course below
  (the rule every dry-stone builder keeps). The last course is the
  chevron band: thin slabs set slanting, each tapped to lean left or
  right until the zigzag runs true. A wall that stands shows the craft;
  unlike the lockers (`slotPlacement`) or the fresco (`overlay`), every
  block's place depends on the course below.
- **The key (`identify`).** Mauch's splinter under the hand lens, and a
  key to woods: pairs of statements to choose between by looking ("pores
  in the cross-section" or "no pores, only even rows of cells"; "dark
  heartwood" or "pale"; "scented" or not). Each choice leads on, to the
  next pair or to a name. A wrong turn ends at a wood whose description
  the splinter contradicts (cedar has no pores; this does), and the key
  sends the player back to the pair that went wrong. Unlike the file of
  sources (`sources`): nothing is sorted, a single path is walked.

## The seal

A new form, **`cartouche`**: the ornamented title panel of an old map of
Africa, where "Monomotapa" and "Ophir" were once written over this
plateau; the player writes the truth inside it.

"Built by {the ancestors of the Shona} from {the 11th century}; the
lintel Mauch called cedar was {tambootie}."

Decoys: the Queen of Sheba, the Phoenicians, King Solomon, Ophir, cedar of
Lebanon, the 10th century BC, Karl Mauch, Cecil Rhodes.

**Ending:** "Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona. In
1980 a country took its name; in 2026 the last of its stone birds came
home."

## Echoes and living things

- **Echoes:** a builder carrying a block on the shoulder, by the breach
  (from the city's time); a hunter with a rifle at the edge of the camp
  (faceless, unnamed).
- **Living things (Masvingo, September):** msasa trees with red new
  leaves, a fig tree rooted in the wall, dry grass, a bateleur eagle high
  overhead (the `eagle` creature), a lizard on the warm granite (the
  `gecko` creature).
