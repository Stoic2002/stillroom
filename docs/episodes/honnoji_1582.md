## Premise

Before dawn on the 2nd day of the 6th month of Tenshō 10 (**21 June
1582**), **Akechi Mitsuhide**, one of his own generals, surrounded the
temple of **Honnō-ji** in Kyoto with some 13,000 men. **Oda Nobunaga**,
the most powerful lord in Japan, was staying there with about seventy
attendants. His retainer Ōta Gyūichi's chronicle, the *Shinchō Kōki*,
says he fought with a bow, then a spear, was wounded, withdrew inside as
the temple burned, and took his own life. The Jesuit **Luís Fróis** wrote
that of the man who made everyone tremble "there did not remain even a
small hair which was not reduced to dust and ashes". His body was never
found. His heir Nobutada died the same day at Nijō. Akechi held Kyoto for
less than two weeks before Toyotomi Hideyoshi defeated him at
**Yamazaki**.

Because no body was found, the **legend** says Nobunaga escaped the fire.
Because no one knows why Akechi turned, more than fifty theories name a
hidden hand: Hideyoshi, Tokugawa Ieyasu, the imperial court. And because
Hideyoshi moved the temple across the city in **1591**, many visitors
today stand at the new Honnō-ji in **Teramachi**, by Nobunaga's memorial,
believing it is where he died.

The old temple stood a kilometre and a half away, a fortified block of
about 120 metres a side between **Rokkaku** and **Shijō-bōmon** streets,
**Aburanokōji** and **Nishinotōin**. In **2007** an excavation there,
where a school had stood, found its moat, and in the moat's sludge
**burnt roof tiles** from the fire.

The keeper's jar holds what is known: Akechi's attack, the fire, and that
nothing of Nobunaga was found.

## Tone and guardrails

- **No violence shown.** The attack and the deaths are told in the
  chronicles' few words. No body, no seppuku shown or described beyond
  "took his own life".
- **Yasuke** (the African retainer Fróis mentions, who fought that day and
  was handed to the Jesuits) appears in a paper, by his own facts, and is
  never used as a decoy for anything.
- **The motive stays unknown.** The seal names the attacker, not a hidden
  hand; Hideyoshi, Ieyasu and the court are decoys, stated as theories.
- **Living people at the dig are not named;** the 2007 work is credited to
  the excavators as a body.
- **Legends are red herrings:** "he escaped", "he died at the Honnō-ji in
  Teramachi", the plotters, Hideyoshi's wooden statue taken for a body.

## Where it is set

- **A. Kyoto in 2007, the dig at the old site** (recommended): the trench
  behind the closed school, the moat's section in its wall, the finds
  room, and across the city the new Honnō-ji in Teramachi with the
  memorial. The chronicle, Fróis's letter and the later papers arrive as
  papers "that should not be here yet", from the past this time. Nothing
  of the attack is shown; the fire is a black layer in the earth.
- **B. The ruins, the morning after (June 1582):** smoke over the black
  timbers, the moat, the city waking. Strong, but close to the search for
  a body, and the dating puzzle would have nothing to date.

## New mechanics (this tale only)

- **The strata (`strata`).** The height of the tale. The moat's fill in
  section, layer over layer, each with its finds in the earth: coins,
  sherds, tiles, a school's bottle cap. Tap a find to read the earliest
  year it could have been dropped (Eiraku coins 1408, Kan'ei coins 1636,
  Imari porcelain 1610s ...). A layer is no older than its youngest find,
  and no layer is older than one beneath it. Give each layer its earliest
  date, and find the layer of the fire: black, full of burnt tile, with
  nothing in it later than 1582, under the first layer with Kan'ei coins.
  One trap: a later pit cuts down through the layers, carrying a modern
  find into an old one. Unlike the snow pit (`snowpit`) or the hair
  (`strand`): nothing is measured; each layer is dated by what it holds.
- **The streets (`streets`).** Kyoto's grid of named streets, and its
  addresses: *agaru* (north of), *sagaru* (south of), *higashi-iru* (east
  of), *nishi-iru* (west of) an intersection. From the chronicle's and
  the leaflet's addresses, mark the block where the temple stood in 1582
  and the one it moved to in 1591, a kilometre and a half apart. Unlike
  the red thread on the Whitechapel map (`thread`): no route is drawn;
  each address names one block, read from its words.

## The seal

A new form, **`marker`**: a city's stone site marker, grey granite, the
words cut into it, the kind that stands at the old site's corner.

"{Akechi Mitsuhide} turned on him, the temple went up in {fire}, and of
Nobunaga {nothing} was found."

Decoys: escaped, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Tokugawa Ieyasu, the imperial court,
a wooden statue, Teramachi, 1591.

**Ending:** "Nobunaga died at Honnō-ji on the 2nd day of the 6th month of
1582, in the old temple between Rokkaku and Shijō-bōmon. Why Akechi
Mitsuhide turned on him, no one knows."

## Echoes and living things

- **Echoes:** an excavator in a hard hat kneeling at the section; a monk
  sweeping the court at Teramachi. No warrior, no Nobunaga, no Akechi.
- **Living things (Kyoto, summer):** cicadas, a crow on the site fence,
  weeds on the spoil heap; at Teramachi, pigeons in the temple court.

## As built

- **Map:** the dig (start) opens on the site office (the cabin on the
  right), the finds room (the cabin on the left) and, through the gate in
  the far fence, today's Honnō-ji at Teramachi.
- **Flow:** the marker and the trench under its tarp (it waits for the
  office). At Teramachi: the hall (the bell), the memorial, the leaflet
  (*Teramachi*, *1591*), the guide's tale (*escaped*). In the finds room,
  the chronicle (*Akechi Mitsuhide*). With the leaflet and the chronicle
  read, the map in the office opens (`streets`): the trench is in the old
  temple's block. Then the section (`strata`, gives *fire*). The tile and
  Fróis's letter then lie in the finds room (*nothing*), and after them
  the later papers on the pinboard (*Hideyoshi*, *Ieyasu*, *the court*,
  *a wooden statue*). The keeper's slip lies behind the memorial once
  those are read; the seal is cut into the stone marker at the dig.
- **The section:** six layers, from the top: topsoil and the school's
  rubble (a ten-yen coin 1951, a one-sen coin 1873: not before 1951);
  brown fill (only an Eiraku coin, 1408, but it lies on later ash: 1636);
  black ash and charcoal (a Kan'ei coin 1636, Imari 1610: 1636); grey silt
  (Imari: 1610); black ash and burnt tiles (Eiraku 1408, a Ming sherd
  1368: 1408); the moat-bottom sludge (Genpō, Song cash, 1078). The fire
  of 1582 is the fifth; the third, the other black layer, is too young.
  The draft's trap of a later pit cut down through the layers became the
  fill's old coin over younger ash: the same lesson (a layer is no older
  than the one beneath it) with nothing to draw.
- **The streets:** eight streets west to east (Aburanokōji to Kawaramachi)
  and seven north to south (Oike to Shijō; Takoyakushi labelled as
  Shijō-bōmon too). The old temple: east of Aburanokōji, between Rokkaku
  and Takoyakushi; today's: east of Teramachi, just south of Oike.
- **Words:** *Akechi Mitsuhide*, *fire*, *nothing* (the answers);
  *escaped*, *Toyotomi Hideyoshi*, *Tokugawa Ieyasu*, *the imperial
  court*, *a wooden statue*, *Teramachi*, *1591* (decoys).
- **Echoes:** an excavator in a hard hat in the trench; a monk sweeping
  at Teramachi (`EchoFigure.excavator`, `EchoFigure.sweeper`). **Living
  things:** a crow on the site fence (`raven`).
- **Audio:** music `kyoto_ash`; effects `temple_bell`, `trowel`;
  interface `strata_tag`, `find_lift`, `street_mark`.

## Things to watch when tested

- Whether "no older than its youngest find, nor than the layer beneath
  it" is understood without the hints, and whether the fill (an old coin
  on younger ash) feels like a fair trap or a trick.
- Whether the finds read at phone size, and whether tapping a find, then
  a layer, then a year is clear; seven year chips may feel like a lot.
- Whether the street names fit in every language (turned along the top,
  two lines down the left), and whether the blocks are easy to tap.
- The dig drawn in depth outdoors (a ground plane, fences running to the
  edges): whether it reads as the same style as Bastille's cell.
- The tone of the chronicle, Fróis and the later papers, for a reader who
  knows the period; the Japanese text and the romanized street names, for
  a native reader.
