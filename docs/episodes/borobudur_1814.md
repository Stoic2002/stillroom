## Premise

**Borobudur** was raised on a hill in the Kedu plain under the
**Sailendra** kings, in the 8th and 9th centuries: nine stacked terraces,
six square and three round, 2,672 relief panels, 504 Buddhas, 72 stupas
of open stonework round a great stupa at the top. By the 11th century
the court had moved east, and the forest grew over it.

The **legend** is that it was lost to the world until **Thomas Stamford
Raffles**, the British lieutenant-governor of Java, "discovered" it in
**1814**. In fact he heard of it on a tour near Semarang and sent an
engineer, **H. C. Cornelius**, who with some two hundred men spent about
forty-five days felling trees and digging away earth. The people of Java
had never forgotten it. The *Nagarakretagama* (c. 1365) names "the vihara
in Budur"; the **Babad Tanah Jawi** tells of a rebel, Mas Dana, taken at
"Redi Borobudur", the hill of Borobudur, in 1709; the **Babad Mataram**,
of a crown prince of Yogyakarta who went to see the thousand statues in
1757 and fell ill on his return.

And the monument kept something back. In **1885** the engineer J. W.
IJzerman found that its broad foot is a casing laid over an older base,
and behind it **160 reliefs** of the *Karmawibhangga*, the law of cause
and effect, deed by deed and fruit by fruit, some with short notes cut
for the carvers. In **1890–91 Kassian Cephas**, the first Javanese
professional photographer, photographed them one panel at a time; then
the casing was put back. Only the south-east corner stands open today.

The keeper's jar holds what is known: who built it, that Java named it
long before 1814, and what its foot still hides.

## Tone and guardrails

- **A living sacred place.** Borobudur is a Buddhist monument still used
  in worship (Waisak). Nothing is climbed on, broken or taken in play;
  the statues are set back where they belong, the casing put back.
- **No violence shown.** The Karmawibhangga's punishments are told as the
  panels' captions, in a word ("a short life"), never drawn.
- **Real people by their facts:** Raffles, Cornelius, IJzerman, Kassian
  Cephas (honoured as the photographer he was), the rebel Mas Dana, the
  crown prince of the Babad. The villagers who did the clearing are
  shown only as echoes and not named.
- **Legends are red herrings:** Raffles "discovered" it; it lay lost under
  Merapi's ash; it was built as a lotus in a lake; the architect
  Gunadharma, who became the Menoreh hills.

## Where it is set

- **A. 1890–91, the hidden foot opened** (recommended): the monument
  cleared but not yet restored, statues fallen from their niches; a
  stretch of the casing taken down at the foot so Cephas can photograph
  the reliefs before it is laid back; the rest house below with the old
  papers. The Babad, Cornelius's report and Raffles's *History of Java*
  (1817) arrive as papers "from the past"; Van Erp, UNESCO and the legends
  as papers "that should not be here yet".
- **B. 1814, Cornelius's clearing:** trees felled, earth dug away, the
  terraces coming out of the hill. Strong for the legend, but the hidden
  foot (the tale's best secret) would be out of reach for seventy years.

## New mechanics (this tale only)

- **The casing (`casing`).** The height of the tale. A stretch of the
  casing at the foot, stone over stone; behind each stone a relief, a
  deed or its fruit. Lift a stone to see what is behind it; only two may
  stand out at once (the casing holds the foot), so they go back unless
  they are a deed and its fruit: then Cephas photographs the pair, and it
  is kept. Find every pair: killing and a short life, anger and
  ugliness, stinginess and poverty, and the rest, as the old text pairs
  them. Unlike anything so far: a memory of what lies where, and a reading
  of what follows from what.
- **The niches (`mudra`).** Fallen Buddhas on the gallery floor, each
  with its hands in a gesture; the monument's four faces, each with empty
  niches. Turn round the monument (east, south, west, north) and set each
  statue in a niche on the face its hands belong to; a few belong to the
  fifth balustrade (teaching) or inside a stupa (turning the wheel).
  Unlike the slots (`slotPlacement`): the statues carry no labels; the
  hands say where they go.

## The seal

A new form, **`lontar`**: palm-leaf strips bound on a cord, the letters
incised and blackened, the kind of leaf Java and Bali wrote on.

"The {Sailendra} kings raised it, the {Babad} named it before Raffles
came, and its {foot} still hides the law of deeds."

Decoys: Raffles, Cornelius, 1814, Gunadharma, a lake, Merapi's ash.

**Ending:** "Borobudur was never lost to Java; it was cleared for the
world in 1814. Behind its foot, 160 reliefs of cause and effect are laid
up again, with only one corner open."

## Echoes and living things

- **Echoes:** a pilgrim walking clockwise in the gallery; a villager of
  1890 carrying a casing stone at the foot. No king, no Raffles is drawn.
- **Living things (Java, the dry season):** swiftlets wheeling round the
  stupas (a new creature, `swifts`), ferns in the joints of the stone, a
  gecko on the rest house wall.
